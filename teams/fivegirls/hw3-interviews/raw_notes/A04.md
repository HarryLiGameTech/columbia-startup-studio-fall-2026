## 1 Opening and NYC routine

**Q1 — PARAPHRASE**

If Starbucks does not work, she usually looks for a larger place such as Target or another large supermarket or shopping center because she expects these places to be more likely to have a restroom.


## 2 Recent restroom experience

### Q2 through Q4 notes

**Q2 — QUOTE**

“The last time was probably when I was downtown with friends. I needed a bathroom, so I looked for a nearby café.”

**Q2 — PARAPHRASE**

She found a café on Google Maps. It looked close and was still open, so she decided to go there.

**Q3 — QUOTE**

“Sometimes you find a place, but you don’t really know what the bathroom is like until you get there.”

**Q3 — QUOTE**

“One time the bathroom was downstairs and the stairs were really narrow.”

**Q4 — QUOTE**

“I had a big bag with me, so going downstairs was kind of annoying.”

**Q4 — PARAPHRASE**

She was still able to use the restroom, but she felt that the route to the restroom was inconvenient.

**Q4 — QUOTE**

“If I had a suitcase or if I had hurt my leg, I probably would have gone somewhere else.”

**Q4 — QUOTE**

“I think the annoying part is that Google Maps tells you where the place is, but not where the bathroom actually is.”

**Q4 — PARAPHRASE**

The situation did not prevent Carrie from using the restroom, but she only learned about the narrow stairs and the bathroom location after arriving at the café.

**INTERPRETATION**

The main issue in this experience was not finding a business with a restroom. The problem was the lack of information about the internal route to the restroom before arriving.

**Q5 — QUOTE**

**Q5 — QUOTE**

**Q5 — PARAPHRASE**

Carrie mainly relies on Google Maps, familiar chains and her memory of places she has previously visited.

Around Columbia, she already knows several reliable restroom locations. In unfamiliar neighborhoods, she prefers predictable chains and larger businesses rather than trying small unfamiliar stores.

**Q5 — QUOTE**

“I don’t think Google Maps really tells you whether the bathroom is upstairs or downstairs, or if there’s an elevator.”


## 4 Missing information

**QUOTE**

“It would be useful to know which floor the bathroom is on.”

**QUOTE**

“I’d also want to know if there are stairs or an elevator.”

**QUOTE**

“And the information should be recent, because an elevator could be broken.”

**INTERPRETATION**

The information Carrie values most includes the location of the restroom inside the building, which floor it is on, whether stairs are required, elevator availability and when the information was last updated.

For Carrie, detailed information appears more useful than simply labeling a location as “accessible.”

## Interviewer reflections

### Interpretation and open questions

**INTERPRETATION:** Carrie does not regularly depend on accessibility features, but route information becomes relevant in temporary situations such as carrying luggage, carrying heavy bags or dealing with an injury.

**INTERPRETATION:** Her current solution is based heavily on predictability. She prefers familiar locations such as Starbucks and larger businesses such as Target because she expects them to have restrooms.

**INTERPRETATION:** Google Maps solves the problem of locating a business but does not fully solve the problem of understanding what happens after entering the building.

**INTERPRETATION:** Specific information such as restroom floor, stairs, elevator availability and last-updated time may be more useful than a general accessible/not-accessible label.

**Open question:** Would people who rely on these accessibility features regularly experience the same information problem more frequently and with greater consequences?
