# NYC restroom interview script

We are testing the two ideas selected in our design work: Find Cleaner Restrooms and Find Accessible Restrooms. We want to learn how people find and use restrooms around NYC, what goes wrong, and whether either concept addresses a problem they already experience. Ask about places they actually visit in the city; campus can be one of those places.

We decided to expand our target users from students on campus to people who live in NYC. This lets us hear from a wider range of people with different routines and restroom needs.

## How to use this script

Use one track per interview. Plan for about 25 minutes: 22 minutes of questions and 3 minutes for extra detail. Ask the main questions first and use the follow-ups only when relevant.

Use shared core questions within each idea so the interviews are easier to compare. Follow-ups can vary. This is our suggested approach; the slides do not explicitly require every teammate to use identical wording.

Cleaner restrooms: interview five people who live in NYC and use restrooms while out in the city. Include people with different neighborhoods and routines, including those who report little difficulty.

Accessible restrooms: interview five people who live in NYC and have firsthand experience needing particular restroom or route features while out in the city. Focus on what they need to reach and use the space; no diagnosis is needed.

Label the interviews C01–C05 for cleanliness and A01–A05 for accessibility. These are ten interview records, with five focused on each problem space. Assign an interviewer and, if possible, a separate note taker. Note each participant’s usual neighborhoods and activities so we can tell whether experiences differ by location or routine.

## What we need to learn

Cleanliness. Does the condition of a restroom change where people go? What time or effort does that cost? What information do they trust, and have they actually reported a problem or posted a review?

Accessibility. Can people find reliable information about the route, entrance, elevator and restroom features they need around NYC? Where does the information fail, and what happens next? Record opening hours and entry restrictions separately from whether the route and restroom physically meet their needs.

## During and after each interview

Let the participant do most of the talking. Ask about one recent event before offering examples or naming our solution. Record whether a concern came up on its own or after a targeted question.

Save exact quotes, actions, time costs and workarounds in the matching file in `raw_notes/`. Mark paraphrases and interviewer interpretations separately.

Record the interview end time. Complete one snapshot within one hour, linking it to the same interview ID and raw notes file.

Basic restroom information is free in our current concept. Ask about past spending, but use referrals and a specific follow-up as commitment signals. These user interviews do not establish whether a business, venue operator or city agency would pay.

## Cleaner restroom interview

Track C — Use for interviews C01 through C05 — About 25 minutes

### 1 Opening

2 minutes

“Hi, I’m \[name\], and I’m part of Five Girls in a Columbia class. We’re learning how people find and choose restrooms around NYC. Could we talk for about 25 minutes? I’m interested in what has actually happened to you, including times when everything worked fine.”

“Please be honest. You can skip any question. Is it okay if we take notes and use anonymous quotes in our class work?”

If recording is planned, ask separately before starting: “Is it okay if I record so I can capture your words accurately?” Record the answer.

### 2 NYC routine

3 minutes

Q1. “Which parts of NYC do you spend time in, and what usually brings you there?”

Q2. “Think about the last time you needed a restroom while out in NYC. Where were you, and how did you decide where to go?”

Follow-up: “Were those familiar places, or did you have to look for them?”

### 3 A recent experience

7 minutes

Q3. “What, if anything, has been difficult about finding or choosing a restroom while out in NYC?”

Q4. “Can you walk me through the most recent time that happened, from when you needed a restroom to what you ended up doing?”

Follow their story: “Where were you?” “What did you do next?” “What made that part difficult?” “About how long did it take?”

Q5. “What happened when you got there? Were you able to use the restroom, and what did you do next?”

If they could not use it, ask what stopped them. Only if clarification is needed, ask about opening hours, a purchase requirement, a key or code, or who was allowed inside. Mark these as prompted details.

Only after the open questions, if cleanliness has not come up: “Has the condition of a restroom ever affected your choice? Tell me about the last time, if there was one.” Mark any answer as prompted.

Q6. “What did that change about your day, if anything?”

If they chose another restroom: “How did you choose it?” “How much extra time or walking did that take?” Ask what “clean” or “dirty” meant in their example.

Q7. “How many times has something like that happened in the past month?”

If there is no problem, record that. Ask what makes their usual choice work and what happened the last time they needed a restroom in an unfamiliar part of NYC.

### 4 Current solutions

4 minutes

Q8. “What, if anything, have you tried to avoid or deal with that situation?”

Ask about the last attempt: “Where did you get information?” “What worked?” “What was missing or wrong?” If they checked a review or recommendation, ask how they judged whether it was current.

Q9. “Have you ever reported a restroom problem or shared information about one? Walk me through the last time.”

If yes: “How did you report it, how long did it take, and what happened afterward?” If no, record no prior reporting rather than asking for a promise to post reviews.

Q10. “Have you spent any money trying to deal with this? What did you pay for, and about how much?”

Also record time or effort spent. If they mention buying something to use a restroom, ask what they bought and how much it cost. Spending nothing is a valid answer; basic restroom search in our concept is free.

### 5 Reaction to the concept

4 minutes — Introduce the concept only now

“We’re exploring a free map of restrooms around NYC that shows recent, timestamped cleanliness reports and lets people report a problem after a visit.”

Q11. “What’s your first reaction?”

Pause and capture their exact words before explaining more.

Q12. “Have you tried anything similar? What happened when you used it?”

Q13. “Thinking about the experience you described, where would this fit, if anywhere? What would still be unresolved?”

Then ask: “What concerns you about relying on those reports?” If needed: “What would you need to know about a report to judge it?” Treat this reaction as an opinion, separate from their past behavior.

### 6 Wrap up

2 minutes

Q14. “What did I miss about this experience, or what is the one thing you most wish had been different?”

Q15. “Do you know one or two people with a relevant experience who might be open to talking? Would you be comfortable introducing us?”

Q16. “Would you be open to a 10-minute follow-up to review an early version? If so, what day or time works?”

“Thanks for your time. Your examples help us understand whether this is worth working on.”

Record whether a referral or follow-up was offered, agreed, received or scheduled. Keep contact details privately. Finish the raw notes and snapshot within one hour of the interview ending.

## Accessible restroom interview

Track A — Use for interviews A01 through A05 — About 25 minutes

### 1 Opening

2 minutes

“Hi, I’m \[name\], and I’m part of Five Girls in a Columbia class. We’re learning how people find restrooms they can use around NYC. Could we talk for about 25 minutes? I’d like to hear about your own experiences, including times when things worked well.”

“Please be honest. You can skip any question, and you don’t need to share medical details. Is it okay if we take notes and use anonymous quotes in our class work?”

If recording is planned, ask separately before starting: “Is it okay if I record so I can capture your words accurately?” Record the answer.

### 2 NYC routine

3 minutes

Q1. “Which parts of NYC do you spend time in, and what usually brings you there?”

Q2. “Think about the last time you needed a restroom while out in NYC. Where were you, and how did you choose which restroom to try?”

Let them describe their routine and the information they checked before suggesting specific access features.

### 3 A recent experience

7 minutes

Q3. “What, if anything, has been difficult about finding a restroom you can use while out in NYC?”

Q4. “Can you walk me through the most recent time you looked for one, from where you started to what happened when you arrived?”

Follow the sequence: “What information did you have before heading there?” “What did you check along the way?” “What happened when you arrived?” “Were you able to use it?”

Q5. “What made that restroom and the route to it work or not work for you?”

Ask them to name the features first. If access details have not come up, ask: “Are there any route or restroom features you need to check?” Use examples such as steps, an entrance, an elevator or stall space only if clarification is needed, and mark the answer as prompted.

If they describe being unable to enter, ask what stopped them. Use examples such as opening hours, a purchase requirement or a locked door only if clarification is needed. Record this separately from barriers in the route or restroom itself.

Q6. “Was anything different from what you expected? What did you do then, and what did it cost you in time or effort?”

If they describe a barrier: “Why did that matter in this situation?” “Did it change where you went or what you could do that day?”

Q7. “How many times has something like that happened in the past month?”

If their routine works, ask what makes it reliable and what happened the last time they needed a restroom in an unfamiliar part of NYC. Record an absence of difficulty without pushing for a problem.

### 4 Current solutions

4 minutes

Q8. “What, if anything, have you used or done to find that information? Tell me about the last time.”

“What did you rely on?” “How did you know it applied to your needs?” “What was missing, unclear or out of date?” Ask about a map, website, person or saved route only after hearing their answer.

Q9. “Have you ever found incorrect access information or reported a change? What did you do, and what happened next?”

If relevant: “How did you decide which source to trust?” “What did you do when you could not verify it?”

Q10. “Have you spent any money trying to find or reach a restroom you could use? What was it for, and about how much?”

Keep the question about this situation. Also record planning time, detours or help needed; spending nothing does not mean there was no barrier.

### 5 Reaction to the concept

4 minutes — Introduce the concept only now

“We’re exploring a free map of restrooms around NYC that shows specific access features, including entrances, elevators, routes and stalls, so people can check what matches their needs before going.”

Q11. “What’s your first reaction?”

Pause and capture their exact words before explaining more.

Q12. “Have you used anything similar? What happened when you used it?”

Q13. “Thinking about the experience you described, where would this fit, if anywhere? What would still be unresolved?”

Then ask: “What concerns you about relying on this information?” If needed: “What would you need to know about its source or last update?” Do not claim our proposed data has already been verified.

### 6 Wrap up

2 minutes

Q14. “What did I miss about this experience, or what is the one thing you most wish had been different?”

Q15. “Do you know one or two people with relevant firsthand experience who might be open to talking? Would you be comfortable introducing us?”

Q16. “Would you be open to a 10-minute follow-up to review an early version? If so, what day or time works?”

“Thanks for sharing your experience. It helps us understand what information people actually need.”

Record whether a referral or follow-up was offered, agreed, received or scheduled. Keep contact details privately. Finish the raw notes and snapshot within one hour of the interview ending.
