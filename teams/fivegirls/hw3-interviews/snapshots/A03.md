## Interview snapshot

**Who:** P4, a Columbia graduate student living near Grand Central. The participant commutes to Columbia four days a week, works in Midtown East, and sometimes visits galleries in Chelsea. On days with knee pain, stairs—especially going down—affect which restroom routes are usable.

**Problem confirmed?** Yes. Before any targeted feature prompt, the participant explained that a general accessibility label does not reveal whether the route to the restroom requires stairs or staff assistance. At a Midtown café, the entrance was level but the restroom was downstairs; the service lift required a manager who was unavailable. The participant left, took a twelve-minute detour, and missed the start of an event (raw notes, 10:15–10:21 AM).

**Key quote:** “The door and the entrance were accessible. The route to the bathroom wasn’t independently accessible at that moment. That difference disappears online.” (raw notes, 10:19 AM)

**Emotional intensity:** 7/10. The participant paused and slowed down while explaining the decision not to risk the stairs. The failed information caused a detour, lateness, additional walking on a painful day, and reduced trust in existing accessibility labels.

**Current solution:** Starts with Google Maps, searches customer photos for entrances and stairs, calls venues when the need is important, saves reliable locations in a neighborhood-based Notes list, and uses New York Public Library branches as backups. These steps are time-consuming and still fail because “accessible” may refer only to the entrance.

**Money spent?** No direct purchase was required for the restroom. The participant later spent about $11 on a cab because of knee pain and said the extra walking contributed, but did not attribute the full fare solely to the restroom detour.

**Solution reaction:** “That’s closer to how I actually make a decision, because ‘accessible’ by itself is almost meaningless.” The participant wanted separate information for the street entrance, interior path, elevator, and restroom itself.

**Biggest concern:** Verification and context. The participant would want a recent photo or information about who verified each feature. A timestamp matters for changing conditions such as elevator outages, while structural facts require a different kind of evidence.

**Referral?** Agreed. The participant offered to forward the interview description to another student who uses a cane. No identifying or contact information was received during the interview.

**Follow-up?** Scheduled for Thursday, October 8, 2026, at 12:00 PM ET, contingent on a clickable prototype being available.

**Surprise:** “Accessible” was not simply wrong in the recent example: the entrance was level, but the bathroom route required an assisted lift. The more useful unit of information may be each segment of the route and whether it can be used independently, rather than one venue-level label.

**Signal:** Green. The participant described an unprompted, recurring information problem with a recent failed attempt, concrete time and mobility consequences, a multi-step workaround, and a prior effort to correct inaccurate map data. The remaining uncertainty is whether sufficiently detailed feature data can be verified and kept current.
