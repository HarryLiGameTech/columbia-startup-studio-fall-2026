Interview snapshot

Who: P1, Columbia undergraduate living in Manhattan. Usually spends time around Morningside Heights, SoHo, the Village and Union Square. Has firsthand experience trying to find usable restrooms while carrying luggage around NYC.

Problem confirmed? Partly. The participant independently described not knowing whether a restroom would actually be usable until arriving. In her most recent example, she abandoned a Starbucks restroom because it was downstairs and she had a heavy suitcase. Her need for route information was situational rather than constant.

Key quote: “The annoying thing is you don’t really know until you get there whether you can actually use it.”

Emotional intensity: 5/10. She described the situation as “really annoying” and estimated that it cost 10–15 minutes, but said it was “not a disaster.”

Current solution: Uses Google Maps and familiar chains such as Starbucks and Whole Foods. In neighborhoods she knows, she remembers places where she has previously used the restroom. Google Maps helps locate businesses but does not reliably show where the restroom is inside the building or what route is needed to reach it.

Money spent? About $5 for an iced tea at the second location in order to use the restroom.

Solution reaction: “Oh, that would actually be useful, especially if it tells you where the bathroom is inside the building.” She specifically wanted detailed information rather than a simple accessible/not-accessible label.

Biggest concern: Information becoming outdated. “Like an elevator could be broken today even if someone said it worked last week.”

Referral? Agreed. Participant said she could ask a roommate with relevant firsthand experience whether she would participate. No introduction received yet.

Follow-up? Agreed but not scheduled. Participant said she would be willing to review a prototype after class the following week.

Surprise: The participant did not normally think of herself as needing an “accessible restroom,” but temporary circumstances such as carrying luggage made route details important. This suggests access information may benefit a broader set of users than we initially assumed.

Signal: Yellow. There is concrete evidence that missing route information caused an abandoned restroom attempt, extra walking and a 10–15 minute delay, but the problem is only occasional for this participant and her strongest support for the proposed solution came after hearing the concept.
