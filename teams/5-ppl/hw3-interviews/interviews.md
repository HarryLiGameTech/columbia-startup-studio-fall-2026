**Interview 2: Participant B for UnlockDrill and ErrorJournal**

Interviewer: Linfeng

Interviewee: Participant B, college freshman, took the SAT last year, scored 1490

Date: Oct 2, 2026

Format: Conversation, about 7 minutes

Interviewer: Okay so before we start, how's college? Is it weird being on the other side of all this?

Interviewee: [laughs] It's so weird. Like a year ago I was the one stressing about the SAT and now I'm just... watching other people do it.

Interviewer: That's exactly why I wanted to talk to you. I'm messing around with an idea for an app — basically a SAT prep thing, but I want it to be more like Duolingo than, you know, a giant textbook. And I'm kind of sick of apps that were obviously built by people who never actually took the test. So I just want to hear what it was actually like for you. No wrong answers, obviously.

Interviewee: Yeah, for sure.

Interviewer: So take me back. When did you actually start studying? Like, real studying, not just thinking about it.

Interviewee: Honestly? I said I'd start in the summer and I did nothing until like... October. So maybe ten weeks before the test, and even then the first month was fake studying.

Interviewer: What's fake studying?

Interviewee: [laughs] Opening a prep book, doing like five questions, checking my phone, closing the book. Feeling productive because I sat down to study. That was most of October.

Interviewer: Okay that's so real. So what actually changed in November?

Interviewee: I took a full practice test and did way worse than I thought. That kind of scared me straight.

Interviewer: So the practice test was the wake-up call. What did you do after that?

Interviewee: I made this kind of schedule? Like, one section a day. But the problem was I could never stick to it. Like I'd plan to do math Monday, then Monday I'd have three hours of homework and by the time I'm done I'm not doing SAT math at 11pm. So then it stacks up and I feel behind and then I just... don't do it.

Interviewer: So the schedule breaks the second a real week happens.

Interviewee: Yeah exactly. And then I'd try to catch up by doing a huge chunk on the weekend, which — honestly I don't think that worked. Like four hours straight, my brain is mush by hour two.

Interviewer: Okay, this is a thing I keep hearing — the big weekend cram. How did that actually feel?

Interviewee: Terrible. And I'd get way fewer right than when I did it in small pieces. I think I was just trying to get it over with.

Interviewer: When you did study well, when was it?

Interviewee: Little bits, honestly. Like on the bus, or waiting for practice to start. Ten, fifteen minutes. Those were weirdly the best ones because there was nothing else to do.

Interviewer: That's really interesting. Was it enough though?

Interviewee: No, I don't think I ever felt like I was doing enough. And the thing is, I knew I could do the questions — I just always felt behind. Like there was never a version of the day where I felt caught up.

Interviewer: Okay so pain point number one: it never fits into a normal day. Let me ask about the actual studying part. When you get a question wrong — what do you do with it?

Interviewee: [pause] So if it's like a practice test, I go through the wrong ones. But if it's a book or a real homework-ish thing, honestly I just look at the answer, go "oh yeah, duh," and move on.

Interviewer: And do you ever see that same mistake come back?

Interviewee: Oh my god, all the time. Like the same grammar thing, or the same stupid mistake where I misread the question. I'll catch it and be like, I knew that.

Interviewer: So the "oh yeah, duh" doesn't actually fix it.

Interviewee: No, it just makes me feel better. [laughs]

Interviewer: Okay, that's a big one. So what would have helped? Like if you got a wrong answer — what do you wish happened?

Interviewee: Hmm. I think if it came back to me later. Like not every wrong one, but the ones I should've gotten. Maybe it just shows up again a few days later and I don't even know it's coming. Like if it's in my phone, I'd just do it.

Interviewer: On your phone — that's the other thing. Be honest, when you're studying, what are you actually doing?

Interviewee: Being on my phone. That's the problem.

Interviewer: So what if the studying was on the phone?

Interviewee: That would've been way better, honestly. Like I'm already on here anyway. But it can't be a boring app. If it feels like a chore I'm gone in two days.

Interviewer: So it has to feel like — what, a game?

Interviewee: Yeah, or at least like, quick. Like I open it, do five things, I'm done. Not sit there and read a whole explanation.

Interviewer: Interesting — you don't want to read explanations?

Interviewee: No, I hate reading the explanations. They're so long. I want it to just... tell me. Like out loud. "You messed up because of this." And I go "oh, okay." Done.

Interviewer: So more like someone talking to you than a wall of text.

Interviewee: Yeah. Like a friend explaining it. My tutor did that and it was way faster than any book.

Interviewer: How much did the tutor thing help, and was it — sorry — expensive?

Interviewee: [laughs] Yeah, it was a lot. My parents did not love it. But it helped, mostly because I couldn't ignore it. Like someone was there, so I couldn't fake it.

Interviewer: So accountability. If an app could kind of do that — check in on you, keep you honest — would that have worked for you?

Interviewee: Maybe? It's not the same as a person. But if it actually knew me — like knew what I kept getting wrong — that's kind of like having a tutor. That part I don't need a human for.

Interviewer: Okay last couple things. If you could go back and change one thing about how you studied, what is it?

Interviewee: I wouldn't have waited. And I wouldn't have done the giant weekend things. I'd just do a little every day and actually deal with the stuff I got wrong instead of going "oh yeah duh."

Interviewer: And if I built this thing — what's the one thing it has to do or you'd never use it?

Interviewee: It has to take two minutes. If it takes two minutes and it's a little fun, I'm there. If it's twenty minutes of work I already have homework.

Interviewer: That's perfect. Anything else before we wrap?

Interviewee: Just — make it not feel like homework. That's it.

Interviewer: That's the whole point. Thank you, this was actually great.

Interviewee: Anytime.
