# Season-long calls: how to write a prediction your group can settle in April

On opening night, someone in every group chat will make a call about the season. "Easy repeat." "No chance, the East is better now." "Best record in the league, write it down."

Nobody will write it down. And in April, when the regular season ends, everyone will remember having said the right thing.

We are a student team at Columbia building CallIt, a shared record of a group's predictions. Before writing code, we talked to friend groups and read how people handle friendly predictions online. A common problem is not the final result. It is remembering what everyone agreed to in the first place.

A call made in October and judged in April has months to drift. The exact wording gets buried in chat. People remember different versions. An injury, trade, or tie in the standings can raise questions nobody planned for. A screenshot helps preserve a message, but it does not define what the group agreed would count.

## Four things to write down on day one

**1. The question, phrased so it has one answer.** "The Knicks will be good" is a mood, not a call. "Will the Knicks finish with the best record in the East?" can only go one way.

**2. The choices.** For a yes-or-no question this is easy. For "Who finishes first in the East?", list the teams people believe in and make sure the options do not overlap.

**3. The closing time.** Answers close before the outcome starts to show. For a season call, close them before the first game, not after a hot first week.

**4. What counts, including the source.** This is the line that ends arguments in April. "Best record means the final regular-season standings on NBA.com. A tie counts as no." Name the source while nobody knows who it helps.

## A worked example

- **Question:** Will the Knicks finish with the best record in the Eastern Conference?
- **Choices:** Yes, No.
- **Answers close:** Before the Knicks' first game.
- **What counts:** The final regular-season standings on NBA.com. A tie for first counts as No.

Everyone answers once. After submission, the answer is locked, and so are the terms. When the season ends, the group's manager checks the standings, records the result, and the group sees who called it. Nobody has to dig through chat, and nobody can quietly upgrade an old take.

The same four lines work for anything long. Will a show's finale reveal the killer? Will a new place on the block still be open in March? Will an apartment be ready by move-in day? If the call will take weeks to settle, write the terms down when you make it.
