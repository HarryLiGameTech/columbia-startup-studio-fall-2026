# Synthesis Comparison

## Part A: Our Synthesis

We reviewed interviews covering two initially separate concepts: a personalized college-list tool and a one-page school-profile tool. Our discussion led us to combine them. The interviews suggest that international students do not experience college selection, school research, and application preparation as separate problems. They experience one continuous process in which they must understand their own position, learn what different schools offer, decide what is realistic, and then complete the required application work.

### Key Patterns Across Interviews

#### 1. The central problem is unclear positioning, not simply a lack of information

Many participants could find rankings, acceptance rates, tuition figures, and school websites. The harder task was translating general information into a decision about themselves. Participants struggled to judge whether a school was realistic, whether it fit their interests and circumstances, and how to classify it as a reach, target, or safety. This uncertainty also appeared in questions about majors, campus culture, location, safety, cost, and the kind of school experience they actually wanted.

This means that a useful product cannot only return a list of schools or facts. It must help students understand how their academic background, interests, budget, and preferences relate to each recommendation, while making clear that admissions outcomes cannot be predicted with certainty.

#### 2. The current research process is fragmented and repetitive

Participants moved among official university websites, U.S. News, QS, Niche, Google, social media, Reddit, school counselors, older students, and AI tools. Official websites were trusted for facts but were often difficult to navigate and structured differently from one school to another. Third-party sources made comparison easier but could be outdated, inconsistent, subjective, or influenced by agencies. Some students saved screenshots or built their own spreadsheets because no single source organized the information they needed.

Students therefore need a consistent starting point that brings together the same categories for every school, including admissions difficulty, academic strengths, cost, scholarships, location, deadlines, international-student support, student experience, and major trade-offs. The platform should still make it easy to open and verify the original sources.

#### 3. Trust matters more than speed

Before the interviews, we assumed that students primarily wanted to save time and that AI could automate much of the application preparation. The interviews changed this assumption. Students were generally willing to spend time researching an important decision, but they often did not know where to begin or which information to trust. Many preferred to investigate and make the final decision themselves.

Their recurring concerns were inaccurate or outdated data, unclear sources, algorithmic bias, false precision, privacy, and recommendations that oversimplified international applicants. Speed is valuable, but only after the platform establishes trust. Important facts and recommendations should include source links, update dates, an explanation of the reasoning, and an honest indication of uncertainty.

#### 4. Students need different levels of help depending on their preparation maturity

At first, we noticed that some students who applied to many schools appeared to need less outside help, while some who applied to fewer schools seemed much more confused. We do not interpret application count as the direct cause of this difference. Instead, it appears to be a rough and imperfect signal of how mature a student's research and application process already is.

Experienced, self-directed applicants often already had a repeatable method. Their main problem was repeatedly searching for the same information and comparing it across schools. Less-prepared applicants did not simply want to apply to fewer schools; they often lacked a clear understanding of their own position, the differences among schools, and the steps in the application process. They needed guidance before they could work efficiently. There were exceptions in both directions, so the product should identify the student's level of preparation instead of assuming it from the number of schools on the list.

This suggests two related product modes: an efficiency and comparison mode for experienced applicants, and a more guided positioning and planning mode for students who do not know where to begin.

#### 5. The product should begin before application season

One participant explained that personalized guidance would have been more valuable earlier in high school, when it could have helped establish GPA, language-score, and other preparation goals. This shifted our view of the product from an application-season shortcut to a longer-term guidance platform. Students may need to understand schools and admissions expectations early enough to act on that information, rather than discovering requirements only when applications are due.

#### 6. Cost, financial aid, and non-ranking factors are essential

Tuition, scholarships, living costs, and financial aid repeatedly affected whether a school was worth further research or an application. Participants also emphasized location, campus environment, safety, student life, international-student support, and employment outcomes. Rankings were commonly used as an entry point, but several participants explicitly wanted to look beyond rankings. A useful recommendation must explain practical and experiential fit, not merely academic prestige.

### Contradictions and What They Might Mean

#### Students want consolidation, but they do not want to surrender control

Participants liked the idea of seeing important information in one place, yet many still wanted to verify tuition, deadlines, admissions requirements, and other important facts on official university websites. This is not a rejection of the platform. It means the platform should function as a transparent starting point and comparison layer, not as an unquestionable authority.

#### Students are interested in AI assistance, but some distrust AI judgment

Some participants described the proposed tools as useful, convenient, or worth trying. Others raised concerns about privacy, algorithmic bias, unfair treatment of different backgrounds, and inaccurate recommendations. These responses suggest that AI should organize information, explain options, and support planning while leaving the final decision to the student. The platform should minimize unnecessary personal-data collection and explain how submitted information is used.

#### Application volume did not consistently predict need

Some high-volume applicants appeared relatively self-sufficient because they already had counselors, family support, or an established research method. Some lower-volume applicants described stronger confusion because they lacked a process and did not know how to position themselves. However, several students with larger lists also reported feeling directionless, while at least one student who researched only a few schools reported low urgency. The meaningful distinction is therefore preparation maturity and access to guidance, not application count alone.

#### Rankings were both useful and insufficient

Participants repeatedly used U.S. News, QS, Niche, and admissions statistics to begin their research. At the same time, they wanted more information about learning environment, location, culture, safety, student life, and personal fit. Rankings help students discover and roughly compare schools, but they cannot answer whether a student will be happy or successful there.

#### Positive reactions did not always translate into willingness to pay

Several participants liked the concept or agreed to test a prototype, but many would not immediately pay $10. Some no longer needed the product because they had finished applying; others wanted to see evidence of quality first or felt that official websites were sufficient. Only a small number expressed immediate purchase interest. This suggests real interest in the problem but limited evidence of validated paid demand. Pricing and willingness to pay require testing with current applicants after they use a working prototype.

### Surprises and Changes in Our Thinking

Our largest change in thinking concerned time. We began with the assumption that the main cost was the amount of time required to prepare applications, and we imagined using AI to complete tasks more quickly. The interviews showed that students are often willing to invest substantial time because college choice is important. Their deeper problem is that they lack a clear starting point, a reliable path through the information, and confidence in the sources and recommendations they encounter.

We were also surprised that students generally preferred to investigate and decide for themselves rather than let AI make the decision. This changed the role we envision for AI. It should not act as an admissions oracle or generate unexplained probability scores. It should help students organize evidence, understand trade-offs, identify missing information, and plan their next steps.

Another important surprise was how early the problem begins. A student who discovers target-school requirements only during application season has little time to change grades, language scores, activities, or preparation. The platform could provide greater value by helping students understand possible goals and requirements earlier in high school.

Finally, the interviews showed that our original two ideas were parts of the same user journey. A personalized recommendation is not credible without detailed, current school information, while a one-page school profile is less useful if it does not connect the information to the student's circumstances. This is why we decided to merge the concepts.

### Overall Read on the Data

The interviews support building a transparent, all-in-one guidance platform for international students. The platform should help students understand their own position, explore and compare schools, build a balanced list, prepare earlier, and navigate the application process. It should combine personalized guidance with standardized school profiles rather than treating those as separate products.

AI should assist with organization and explanation rather than make decisions for students. Every important recommendation should show its reasoning, source, and update date, and the interface should make uncertainty visible. The product should also adapt to the student's preparation maturity: experienced applicants may want fast comparison and workflow support, while uncertain applicants need more structured positioning and step-by-step guidance.

The evidence confirms meaningful problems around positioning, fragmentation, and trust, but it does not yet prove strong willingness to pay. Most interviewees had already completed the application process, and many had never paid for research or school-list tools. Our next validation should therefore involve current high-school applicants using a prototype. We should measure whether they can identify appropriate schools more confidently, begin useful preparation earlier, and complete research or application steps without losing control of the decision.

## Part B: AI Synthesis

### Method and Evidence Limits

This synthesis treats the 12 interview records as two related but differently prompted sets. Interviews A01-A06 focused on building a personalized and balanced college list. Interviews B01-B06 focused on researching schools and the proposed one-page school profile. Because the interview guides were different, a theme appearing more often in one set may partly reflect what participants were asked.

Eleven files contain dialogue-level interview records. B06 contains only a researcher-written summary, not a raw transcript. B06 is included where its summary supports a pattern, but it is excluded from the exact-quote section and treated more cautiously than the other records. Several emotional-intensity ratings in the snapshots were assigned by the interviewer rather than reported by participants; those ratings are used only as supporting context.

### Patterns Across Interviews

#### 1. Students assemble decisions from fragmented sources

This is the broadest cross-interview pattern. Participants described moving among official university websites, rankings, search engines, social media, student communities, counselors, older students, and AI tools. No source consistently provided both reliable facts and enough context for a decision.

- **Strong evidence:** A01 described the process as time-consuming and found inconsistent or old admissions information across websites; its snapshot rated the emotional intensity 7/10. A04 described scattered and conflicting information while completing the process without experienced help; its snapshot also estimated 7/10. B03 described a Google-to-U.S. News-to-official-site workflow, saved screenshots on a phone, and repeatedly verified information.
- **Moderate evidence:** A05 said information was spread across official sites, third-party platforms, and student discussions; its snapshot estimated 6/10. B04 repeatedly searched the same categories across nonstandardized university sites; its snapshot estimated 5/10. B02 had to browse many sites and look through each university individually; its snapshot estimated 4/10.
- **Milder evidence:** B05 called the information scattered but did not report serious difficulty with unreliable information; its snapshot rated intensity 4/10. B01 felt there was too much information but said this did not affect the final decision; its snapshot rated intensity 2/10.
- **Qualified supporting evidence:** B06's summary describes repeated searches across university pages, especially for tuition, scholarships, and financial aid, but no raw dialogue is available to assess intensity.

The common need is not simply “more information.” It is a way to locate, compare, and verify information without rebuilding the same research process for every school.

#### 2. General admissions data do not answer the personal-fit question

This pattern is strongest in the A interviews. Participants could often find acceptance rates, rankings, and average scores, but struggled to translate those figures into a judgment about their own chances or fit.

- **Strong evidence:** A06 said they did not know what kind of school they wanted and felt like a “headless fly,” relying heavily on rankings and familiar names. A01 could not independently judge reach, target, or safety and described uncertainty about whether their background was competitive. A04 lacked firsthand campus experience and relied on scattered information and gut feeling; the process was described as stressful.
- **Moderate evidence:** A05 distinguished a school's general acceptance rate from its relevance to an international applicant with a specific background. A03 compared language score, GPA, prior students' results, and published admissions data, and wanted personalized targets.
- **Mixed evidence:** A02 relied on family and school for reach, target, and safety judgments but later named those recommendations as unnecessary or unclear. The response does not establish whether the participant rejected the concept itself or found the description unclear.

The interviews support two separate jobs: estimating whether a school is realistic and judging whether the school is desirable. The records do not support treating those as the same question.

#### 3. Trust depends on provenance, recency, and visible uncertainty

Trust concerns recur across both interview sets, but they take different forms: outdated facts, conflicting data, algorithmic bias, sponsorship, privacy, missing disadvantages, and false precision.

- **Strong evidence:** A01 wanted the basis of recommendations, the algorithm, and the information sources. A02 feared bias and was reluctant to enter personal information. A04 raised algorithmic bias and wanted evidence that the product was not sponsored and had been tested. A05 wanted source links, update dates, recommendation logic, and an indication of uncertainty. B03 and B04 wanted official links and last-updated dates and expected to verify consequential information. B06's summary reports strict official verification and direct confirmation with admissions offices.
- **Moderate evidence:** A03 wanted official, annually updated data and would use the tool as an assistant rather than the only decision source. B02 initially reported no trust concern but said source links would affect trust and would still verify application requirements. B05 would double-check tuition, requirements, and deadlines.
- **Counterexample:** B01 reported no major concern, said visible sources would be sufficient, and would not re-check the summary. This was a low-intensity interview in which the final choice was driven by scholarship value.

Trust is therefore not a single requirement. Some users want basic source visibility; others want to audit the recommendation logic and understand uncertainty. The data do not support assuming that all users will verify information in the same way.

#### 4. Cost is both a school-selection criterion and a product-pricing constraint

Tuition, living costs, scholarships, financial aid, and application fees appeared throughout the interviews.

- A01 worried about tuition, aid for international students, and wasting application fees on unrealistic schools.
- A02 identified tuition as more difficult than rankings but declined to explain why.
- A03 removed a school partly because it was too expensive.
- A05 considered estimated cost and the cost of adding applications.
- A06 considered tuition and applied to WashU after receiving an application-fee waiver.
- B01 repeatedly checked tuition and ultimately chose the school offering the larger scholarship.
- B02 repeatedly checked tuition and wanted scholarship information added.
- B03, B04, and B05 included tuition or cost among repeatedly researched facts.
- B06's summary describes financial-aid and scholarship information as especially vague and reports that tuition and scholarships would always be independently verified.

The pattern is strong in prevalence, although emotional intensity varies. The interviews show that affordability can change the school decision itself. They do not show an equally strong willingness to pay for a research product.

#### 5. Official facts and lived experience require different sources

Participants consistently treated official university websites as the most credible source for deadlines, tuition, requirements, and program availability. However, official sites were not considered sufficient for student life, campus culture, safety, learning environment, or whether a school felt personally suitable.

A02 wanted more information about the learning environment instead of only rankings. A04 used social media and the international-student community and wanted students to look beyond reputation. A05 used student-created content for experience while trusting official sites for facts. A06 looked at Reddit for fit and considered campus culture and safety. B01 used Rate My College for student life but worried about unreliable reviews. B02 preferred speaking with someone who attended the school. B03 preferred older students to agency-influenced social posts. B04 looked for patterns across several student sources. B06's summary says the participant contacted current students directly.

This pattern is not evidence that one source type should replace the other. Participants used official and experiential sources for different questions.

#### 6. Most participants positioned the proposed tool as a starting point, not a final authority

A03 explicitly described the tool as an assistant. A05 said it could create an initial list but would not replace counselor judgment or personal research. B03 would use a summary first and then verify important information. B04 would use it to decide whether deeper research was worthwhile. B05 would use it to screen schools faster but still check official sources. B02 would use the page to understand what to search for and then verify application requirements.

There are exceptions. A04 believed an all-in-one product could address nearly all of the difficulties described. B01 said they would not re-check information if sources were visible. These differences may reflect trust style, access to guidance, or how much friction the participant experienced, but the interviews do not establish the cause.

#### 7. Positive concept reactions are stronger than evidence of paid demand

Most participants reacted positively to one of the proposed tools, but payment behavior was much weaker.

- **Immediate $10 willingness:** A03 and B03.
- **Conditional or price-sensitive:** A01 wanted to know whether $10 covered a month or an application season. A04 said $10 would have been fair when the need was active but would not buy now. A05 and B04 wanted to see a strong prototype or sample first. B02 considered approximately $5 more acceptable. B05 might pay only if the time savings were substantial.
- **No purchase:** A02 said the product was not needed. B01 declined because the participant expected to graduate early.
- **Not established:** A06 was willing to test a prototype, but willingness to pay was not asked. B06's summary reports an estimated willingness to pay of approximately $50, but the absence of raw dialogue prevents checking the wording or conditions.

No participant who was asked reported previously paying for a dedicated college-research or school-list tool. Some had free school counseling, and others avoided agencies because of cost. The records support interest and perceived usefulness, but they do not establish broad paid demand.

### Contradictions

#### Personalized recommendations were valuable to some and questionable to others

A01, A03, and A05 valued help with realistic reach, target, and safety categories. A06 wanted recommendations connected to personal experiences and interests. A02, however, named reach, target, and safety recommendations as unnecessary or unclear and worried that the system would be biased. One possible explanation is that some participants wanted help interpreting their profile, while others trusted existing family or school judgments or considered algorithmic classification risky. The interviews do not establish which explanation applies to A02.

#### Participants disagreed about entering personal data

A04 and A06 were comfortable entering academic and preference information. A01 was willing if the information would not be disclosed. A05 was comfortable only with necessary information and a clear privacy policy. A02 was reluctant because of both privacy and unfair-judgment concerns. These may represent different privacy sensitivities or different beliefs about the benefit received in exchange for data.

#### Verification behavior ranged from strict to minimal

B03, B04, B05, and B06 expected to verify important facts against official sources. A03 and A05 also resisted treating recommendations as definitive. B01, by contrast, said they would not re-check a sourced page, while B02 would verify application requirements despite initially reporting no trust concern. This may reflect different risk tolerance, research habits, or past exposure to inaccurate information.

#### Rankings were necessary anchors and insufficient decision tools

Rankings were a starting point for A01, A02, A03, A04, A06, B01, B02, B03, and B05. Yet A02, A04, A05, A06, B02, and B04 also emphasized learning environment, location, culture, safety, student life, or personal fit beyond rankings. These positions are not necessarily mutually exclusive: the contradiction may reflect using rankings for discovery while using other evidence for final choice. The interviews do not show whether participants could consistently make that transition.

#### Information overload was consequential for some and minor for others

A01 and A04 described high frustration, while A05, B03, and B04 reported meaningful repeated work and uncertainty. B01 also said there was too much information but reported that it did not affect the final decision because scholarship value dominated. B02 and B05 described inconvenience without high emotional intensity. This may reflect differences in available support, the complexity of the decision, or the presence of a single dominant criterion such as cost.

#### Interest did not consistently lead to purchase intent

A04 called the idea “brilliant” but would not buy it now because the need had passed. A05 and B04 found the concept useful but wanted evidence before paying. B01 called it convenient but declined both follow-up participation and purchase. A02 found it interesting but said it was not needed. Positive reactions may indicate concept appeal without urgency, current relevance, or confidence in product quality.

### Surprises

- **A03 moved the use case earlier than application season.** The participant wanted personalized targets in the first year of high school so that GPA and language-score goals could guide later preparation.
- **A06's problem was partly self-definition.** The participant did not only lack school facts; they did not know what kind of school they wanted. More information alone would not necessarily solve that problem.
- **B01's preferred improvement was direct access to admissions officers.** Although the interview focused on organizing school information, the participant wanted communication with admissions offices to be simpler.
- **B03 had already built a lightweight comparison workflow with phone screenshots.** The desired product extension was an automatically generated Excel-style comparison, not only a more attractive school profile.
- **A02 wanted learning-environment information while distrusting personalized recommendations.** This suggests interest in richer school understanding without acceptance of algorithmic fit judgments.
- **B06's reported $50 valuation is an outlier.** It is much higher than the other responses, but the raw wording and conditions cannot be checked because B06 contains only a summary.

### Tentative User Segments

These segments are provisional because the sample is small, all participants had already completed the application process, and the two interview guides emphasized different problems.

#### 1. Direction-seeking applicants

**Most visible in:** A01, A04, and A06.  
These participants had difficulty judging realistic options, understanding fit, or knowing how to proceed. They showed stronger frustration than most of the sample and wanted explanations or guidance, not only facts. A01 had a counselor but lacked school-specific context; A04 handled the process independently; A06 had an advisor but still felt disconnected from the schools.

#### 2. Structured researchers who want an auditable assistant

**Most visible in:** A03, A05, B03, B04, and B06.  
These participants already had a research method, counseling access, or a verification habit. They would use a product to create an initial list, standardize comparisons, or reduce repeated work, but wanted source links, update dates, and the ability to verify consequential facts. Their need is efficiency and decision support rather than full delegation.

#### 3. Convenience-oriented, price-sensitive researchers

**Most visible in:** B01, B02, and B05.  
These participants recognized some friction and liked consolidated information, but reported low-to-moderate intensity. Cost, scholarships, or a limited set of practical facts often dominated their decisions. They were reluctant to pay $10 or saw payment as conditional on clear time savings.

#### 4. Trust-sensitive reluctant adopter

**Most visible in:** A02.  
This participant had used several existing sources and had family or school support, but worried about fairness, bias, and privacy, was reluctant to provide personal information, and declined to pay because the tool was not needed. One interview is not enough to establish a durable segment, but it identifies a failure mode that broader concept testing should include.

## Part C: Comparison

The AI analysis surfaced several details that our synthesis did not emphasize as clearly. It separated the need for official facts from the need for lived student experience, showing that students use university websites for requirements and costs but turn to older students, social media, and communities for culture and daily life. It also examined the gap between positive reactions and paid demand more systematically: many participants liked the concepts, but only A03 and B03 clearly agreed to pay $10, while most others were conditional, price-sensitive, no longer needed the product, or were not asked. The AI also treated source-checking behavior as a spectrum rather than assuming every student verifies information in the same way. Finally, it identified several smaller or outlying needs, including direct communication with admissions officers, spreadsheet-style comparison, and the unusually high valuation reported in B06. We checked all five quotes selected by the AI against the original interview files and found exact matches. B06 was excluded from the quote list because its file contains only a summary rather than a complete raw transcript.

Our synthesis made a product-level connection that the AI analysis did not make on its own. We concluded that the personalized college-list concept and the one-page school-profile concept should be combined because they address different stages of the same user journey: students need both trustworthy school information and help applying that information to their own position. We also placed more weight on the change in our original assumption about time. We began by assuming students mainly wanted AI to complete application work faster, but concluded that many students were willing to spend time researching; their deeper difficulty was not knowing where to begin or what to trust. From our discussion, we further interpreted differences in application behavior as differences in preparation maturity, leading us to envision a guided path for uncertain applicants and a comparison-and-efficiency path for experienced applicants. The AI identified related segments and cautioned that application count alone does not predict need, but it did not turn those observations into the same combined product direction.
