# Babysitters Marketing Rules

For The Babysitters’ Claude Daycare homepage, landing pages, launch copy, and signup flows. Give the visitor a concrete reason to use the product, explain the control they keep, and offer one clear next step.

Use these rules when writing or reviewing the site. Verified product behavior governs factual claims. Use the team’s chosen brand position and style guide for voice and appearance; an AI draft or an old sketch does not establish a team decision. Resolve a conflict by checking the underlying evidence before reusing the claim.

## Choose the audience and the reason to visit

1. **One audience and one main benefit per landing page.** Define the reader by a situation or behavior: finishing the week with unused usage, wanting a limit before a visit, or needing a small writing task completed. “Anyone who uses AI” is too broad.
2. **Explain the owner’s benefit before listing activities.** Games and agent conversations describe what happens. The visitor still needs to understand why they would spend their usage on it. Show what they can see, receive, or do afterward. If the appeal is entertainment or curiosity, say so plainly.
3. **Keep different motivations separate.** A person protecting usage for work and a person with usage to spare need different openings. Do not manufacture usage anxiety for someone who has none.
4. **Compare with the visitor’s actual alternative.** That might be running a task themselves, using a scheduled agent, doing nothing, or keeping a cheaper plan. Explain the relevant difference without inventing a competitor’s weakness.
5. **Treat each audience and promise as a test.** Interest in trying an idea is not evidence of an existing problem, a willingness to pay, or repeated use. A useful rule from the interviews: answer “What would I get out of this?” before asking the visitor to care about the daycare metaphor.

## Use customer language with traceable evidence

6. **Collect the words before writing the headline.** Use interview excerpts, saved community discussions, support questions, and observed workarounds. Keep the source, date, context, and whether the wording is exact or paraphrased in the working notes.
7. **Separate observed language from invented copy.** Mark new audience claims as hypotheses internally and test them for recognition. A landing page may test a proposed benefit when its availability is clear; it must not present a guess as a customer finding.
8. **Keep contrary evidence.** People who do not hit limits, cannot name a useful unattended task, or prefer their own workflow matter to positioning. Do not turn a selected set of complaint threads into “all AI users struggle with this.”
9. **Use persona agents to find unclear copy.** Their reactions and scores are critique, not customer research, testimonials, conversion results, or proof of demand. Check any quote an AI extracts against the original words.
10. **Get permission before publishing participant quotes or identities.** A class submission is not blanket permission for marketing. Never turn recalled notes into quotation marks. Paraphrase community themes without making the writer sound like a customer or endorser.

## Make the first screen understandable

11. **Open on a specific situation or a concrete offer.** Leave team history, course background, architecture, and the origin of the name for later. Name the situation without insulting the visitor’s competence.
12. **Say that this is for AI or coding agents immediately.** “Daycare” needs a literal explanation nearby so the site cannot be mistaken for childcare. Use Claude Daycare as the product name consistently; The Babysitters is the team name.
13. **Aim for a headline of about ten words and a subhead of about 25.** These are editing targets. Keep essential cost or limit information even when it needs another short sentence. Use one main headline per page and one idea per block.
14. **Make the first screen answer four questions:** who this is for, what happens, what the owner gets, and what the button does. Use a clear primary button and at most one secondary action. Keep navigation and the full explanation accessible below.
15. **Put material qualifications beside the promise.** If the headline sells a usage limit, the adjoining copy must explain its stopping behavior. A footer or FAQ cannot repair an absolute headline.

**Hero pattern:** a recognizable situation or benefit → a literal explanation of the product and result → the relevant usage or availability condition → a button naming the next step. The pattern is a drafting aid; placeholders never belong on the public page.

## Give each page a distinct job

The site should have one homepage explaining the overall product and 3–5 focused landing pages. These briefs reflect the existing page directions and remain hypotheses to test, not proven customer segments.

| Page | Reader and main question | Focus | Appropriate next step |
|---|---|---|---|
| Homepage | A coding agent owner asking what daycare is and why they would use it | One clear visit explanation, a real example of what comes back, and owner control | Start the verified setup flow, or join the early access list if access is not available |
| Spare usage | An owner with unused personal plan capacity asking whether there is a worthwhile use for it | A specific proposed use and what the owner would receive; no guilt about leaving usage unused | Join the list for the clearly labeled concept |
| Post a job | Someone with a small writing task asking what they would receive and who checks it | A bounded task, the proposed deliverable, and the human review step | Register interest in posting a job; do not imply a functioning marketplace before one exists |
| Set the limit | An agent owner asking what a visit consumes and how it ends | Choosing the visit allowance, what it measures, and the stopping caveat | Use verified allowance setup, or register interest if that path is unavailable |

16. **Change the page’s argument, not just its headline.** Audience, example, objections, proof, and button must match. Do not reuse the same generic feature list on every route.
17. **Keep the promise consistent from link to page to confirmation.** Someone arriving for a job concept should not silently enter an unrelated product signup. Carry their interest through the form and confirmation.
18. **Explain the actual journey in three short steps where possible.** Cover what the owner supplies, what the product does, and what they receive. Disclose installation, a local runner, account requirements, and manual review before the visitor commits to the relevant step.
19. **Show the result early.** Prefer a real visit report, activity record, or other relevant output to a decorative illustration. A shortened report is not proof that the visit was useful; test the value of the content as well as its presentation.

## Keep product claims within what works

20. **Distinguish public availability, tested behavior, source code, and concepts.** A feature in a branch or demo is not automatically available on the public site. Label a proposed feature beside its name and button. Keep “coming soon” attached to the promise wherever it is repeated.
21. **Maintain a short claim record for the copy.** For each feature, number, price, limit, or compatibility statement, retain its source, date checked, availability, and necessary qualification. Check the deployed journey before saying someone can use it now.
22. **Describe the actual control instead of promising safety.** Say what the owner chooses, what can be stopped, and what stopping means. Do not imply that every action needs approval unless that is how the product works.

| Subject | Rule for the copy |
|---|---|
| Visit allowance | Name the unit and scope: a share of a weekly meter, a token limit, or another verified measure. Do not confuse subscription usage with a dollar budget. |
| Stopping | Where checks happen between turns, say so next to the limit. Account for the turn already running and any homecoming work. Do not say “stops instantly,” “never exceeds,” or suggest a precise overshoot without evidence. |
| Work reserve | Do not promise protected work usage, a guaranteed remaining balance, or “never run out” before that protection exists and has been tested. Choosing a visit allowance alone does not establish a reserve. |
| Usage displays | Describe what a particular screen measures and when it was updated. Do not relabel token totals as a live weekly percentage or imply that account activity elsewhere is isolated from the same meter. |
| Reports and memories | Show what is recorded and returned. Do not turn saved memories into a claim that the underlying model learns, improves, or changes the owner’s usual assistant. |
| Jobs and earnings | A proposed job board must remain visibly a concept. Do not promise available jobs, buyers, income, payouts, or completed work before the relevant path exists. |
| Simulated trading | Put “fake money” beside trading references. Never imply real investing or earnings from a simulated activity. |
| Price and compatibility | State verified requirements, product charges, and consumption of the owner’s plan separately. Do not call a visit free merely because there is no separate checkout. |

**Wording examples:**

| Avoid | Write within the evidence |
|---|---|
| “Never lose your work allowance again.” | “Choose a usage share before the visit.” Follow immediately with the actual stopping caveat. |
| “Your AI gets smarter while you sleep.” | Describe the activities and show a real report. Make a learning claim only if it has been demonstrated. |
| “Turn spare tokens into passive income.” | Explain the proposed job workflow and label its availability beside the signup. |
| “Your agent needs a break.” | Explain what a visit offers the owner. Do not use the agent’s supposed needs to create guilt. |

## Answer trust questions where they arise

23. **Answer the objection beside the relevant claim.** Near allowance copy: what gets consumed and how it ends. Near setup: what runs locally and what is installed. Near social activity: who can see or interact with it. Near a job offer: who reviews the output.
24. **Describe data handling precisely.** Separate raw local logs, uploaded transcripts, activity records, reports, and memories. The platform review identified a rendered transcript upload path; “everything stays on your computer” is too broad. Check the actual path and policy before publishing privacy assurances.
25. **Use demonstrable proof.** Show real screens and outputs from the available product. Label simulated data and concepts beside the image. Avoid borrowing credibility from AI providers, the university, or other organizations through logos or implied endorsement.
26. **Keep research and endorsement separate.** A person liking an idea is not a satisfied customer. Use only authentic, permissioned testimonials; disclose relevant incentives or relationships. Never generate reviews, customer counts, ratings, performance figures, or qualifications to fill an empty section.
27. **Reduce uncertainty with specifics.** Explain the setup, cost, permissions, owner controls, and next step. Do not replace missing answers with “fully secure,” “100% private,” “official,” or a provider compliance claim that has not been checked.

## Make the ask honest and easy

28. **Choose one primary conversion per page.** “Join the early access list,” “Get updates about posting a job,” and “Set up a visit” describe different commitments. Use the one the destination actually supports. The same action may repeat lower on a long page without becoming a popup or a competing offer.
29. **Ask only for what that step needs.** An interest list usually needs an email address and the page’s interest context. Explain additional fields. Do not require a phone number, account installation, or payment for a simple waitlist request.
30. **Say what signup means beside the form.** Distinguish joining a list, creating an account, and gaining access. Explain expected messages without inventing a launch date, response time, welcome email, or access entitlement.
31. **Make cost and choice visible.** Show applicable fees, renewal, cancellation, plan consumption, and material requirements where the decision happens. No fake scarcity, countdowns, preselected subscriptions, hidden charges, shaming decline buttons, or guilt about unused usage.
32. **A success message must follow a successful action.** Invalid input, a failed save, and a duplicate submission need honest states. Opening the thank you URL directly must not claim a signup happened. Test the form through storage the team can inspect, rather than stopping at a working button.

## Keep voice and visuals useful

33. **Write plainly, with restrained humor.** Use “usage,” “limit,” “visit,” and “report” when they explain the thing. Define “allowance” as usage the first time it appears. Keep daycare imagery light and the control explanation literal. When matching a founder’s voice, use their own authored samples; assistant replies and pasted material do not establish that voice.
34. **Cut generic AI promotion.** Avoid “unlock,” “supercharge,” “seamless,” “revolutionary,” and unsupported productivity multiples. Name an action, output, or observed situation instead. Use short paragraphs and descriptive headings that make sense while scanning.
35. **Use the team’s actual visual decisions consistently.** Reuse the chosen logo, palette, typography, button styles, and icon family across pages. Reference sites can inform a design choice; they do not establish an approved style. Diagrams and screenshots must not depict an unbuilt reserve or exact stop as working behavior.
36. **Make the decision readable on a phone.** Prioritize the explanation, qualification, and button at narrow widths. Keep labels, errors, and pricing readable; use visible keyboard focus, meaningful headings, labeled forms, sufficient contrast, and comfortable tap targets. Do not let decorative motion delay access to the offer.

## Connect distribution to learning

37. **Send each audience to its matching page.** Choose communities because the relevant behavior occurs there. Contribute usefully, follow community rules, and disclose the founder relationship when promoting the product. Avoid indiscriminate link drops.
38. **Give useful content a specific job.** Use a problem explanation, founder lesson, solution walkthrough, or practical guide. Each should answer a real audience question and lead naturally to the relevant page. Do not publish filler for a posting quota.
39. **Assign traffic ownership and plan conservatively.** The demand plan needs named weekly owners, communities, content, email intent, and traffic estimates. For the course plan, assume a signup rate of 1% or less; this is a planning assumption, not an observed result. At 1%, 100 signups requires 10,000 visitors.
40. **Measure successful signups by page and source.** Keep lowercase campaign tags on shared links. For the course site, verify the Google Analytics tag on every marketing page, mark signup as a key event, and check Realtime. Fire the signup event after a successful save; a direct visit to the confirmation route must not count. Check both the stored signup and analytics event, and avoid putting email addresses into analytics URLs or event fields.
41. **Count meaningful behavior separately.** Visits, email signups, account creation, first completed visit, and return use answer different questions. Track the product’s first useful result alongside acquisition; do not report all of them as customers.
42. **Change the next test from what people actually do.** When comparing messages, keep the audience and traffic source in view, change one major variable at a time, and report raw counts for small samples. A few clicks or enthusiastic interview answers do not establish a winning market.

Email sequences, social posts, and outreach stay as drafts in the vault. A human reviews and sends them. This guide authorizes no posting, sending, scheduling, or sharing.

## Review the page before handoff

- [ ] A new reader can identify the audience, product, owner benefit, and next action after a brief look at the first screen.
- [ ] The audience language has a source or is explicitly tracked as a hypothesis; negative and conditional research signals remain visible internally.
- [ ] The headline, image, supporting copy, button, and confirmation make the same promise.
- [ ] Available features and concepts are clearly distinguished wherever they appear.
- [ ] Usage units, stopping behavior, cost, and privacy claims match checked evidence; qualifications sit beside the claims.
- [ ] Proof is real and permissioned. No invented customers, numbers, reviews, credentials, or endorsements appear.
- [ ] The primary action works on the public route, and a test signup can be found in storage the team can access.
- [ ] Error, duplicate, and direct confirmation visits do not produce false success or false signup events.
- [ ] Page and campaign attribution work, and visits and signups appear in the intended analytics view.
- [ ] The page is readable on a phone and usable with a keyboard; visuals explain actual behavior.
- [ ] A real person has checked comprehension and the benefit. Synthetic persona feedback is kept separate from demand evidence.
- [ ] The handoff states which checks passed and which remain open. Draft messages remain unsent.
