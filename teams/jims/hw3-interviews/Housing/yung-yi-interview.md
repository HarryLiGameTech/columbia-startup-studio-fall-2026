# Interview Notes: Yung Yi (NYC Housing)

## Interview overview

- **Interviewer:** Jordan
- **Interviewee:** Yung Yi, a Columbia University affiliate
- **Focus:** Finding short-term or sublet housing in New York City, from the initial search through signing.
- **Why a sublet:** Yung Yi might move to a different city, so a full lease was not a good fit.

## Search timeline and platforms

- Began casually searching in July; urgency increased through August.
- Used platforms in roughly this order of frequency:
  1. Columbia Sublets, trusted because postings were limited to students and professors.
  2. A sublet-specific site Yung Yi found during a prior summer search, with a popular and easy map interface.
  3. A second sublet site discovered through an Instagram ad.
  4. Facebook groups, used frequently but with poor experiences.
  5. Personal network, which ultimately led to the current place.
- Found the current place through Yung Yi's boyfriend. His parent was in a Columbia student-parents group chat, and the landlord rents only to Columbia affiliates.

## Time investment and tracking

- During August, the most urgent period, Yung Yi spent about 2-3 hours per day browsing, or more than 10 hours per week excluding viewings.
- Checked listings every day and paid extra attention when new postings appeared.
- Did not use a spreadsheet; instead, shared screenshots with family to track options.

## Viewings and decision criteria

- Conducted fewer than eight in-person viewings.
- Listing photos were usually accurate, but they did not always reveal living habits or cleanliness. One apartment was much messier than its photos suggested, and another advertised a room that was misleadingly small.
- Key criteria: overall vibe, a sublessor's guest policy, and timeline fit.
- Asked for time extensions when feeling rushed, such as until the end of the week. Sublessors were generally accommodating.
- Yung Yi's mother advised asking the landlord to hold the apartment before someone else took it.

## Challenges and pain points

### Scam experience

- Was scammed on a platform that promoted safety but did not vet posters.
- The scammer used a fake listing, a US phone number, an impersonated identity, and a fake driver's-license photo.
- Reporting the listing did not lead to a quick takedown.
- Platforms should better protect users from scams, especially when search pressure can lead to rash decisions.

### Guarantor and financial documentation

- As an international student, US-based guarantor requirements were a major obstacle.
- Some buildings required proof of guarantor income at 16x or 90x monthly rent.
- Some treated subletters like full renters and required full documentation.
- Nearly arranged co-guarantors from friends before finding the current place.
- Learned that a Social Security number is not legally required to provide.
- A credit score was generally not an issue if the applicant had a credit card.

### Mental load

- Constantly thinking about housing was one of the hardest parts of the experience.
- The stress contributed to the risk of rash choices, including the scam situation.
- A housing platform could help reduce this mental burden through its design.

## Financial and paperwork experience

- Final costs were largely expected; electricity utility bills were the only extra cost beyond rent.
- Paperwork for the current place was minimal: one PDF to sign, enabled by an informal, trust-based Columbia-affiliate arrangement.

## Advice and reflections

- Get a second opinion from family or friends before sharing personal information with a potential landlord.
- Check listings every day and respond quickly because competition for good apartments is real.
- Knew about brokers and agents but did not want to pay additional fees on top of high NYC housing costs.
- Would consider a professional service only if the situation became truly dire.

## Referral

- Suggested a friend named Nia, described as very good at apartment hunting, as a potential next interviewee.
- Most other friends who have found housing in the city are busy working and may be difficult to reach.
